What statistical analysis does

Descriptive statistics

Summarises the data actually collected: centre, spread and shape.

Mean · Median · SD · IQR · Graphs

Inferential statistics

Uses a sample to estimate or test claims about a larger population.

Confidence intervals · Hypothesis tests

Population · μ

Sample · x̄

Inference

A parameter describes the population; a statistic is computed from the sample. No method can make poor data reliable.

Ordered categories

Supports generalising. Convenience samples are faster but less reliable.

Bias and dependence

10 readings from 20 devices are not 200 independent devices.

Describing data: centre and spread

Mean

Uses every value. For 8, 9, 10, 10, 11, 22 the mean is 11.67.

Median

The middle of the sorted data: 10. The extreme 22 barely affects it.

SD or IQR

Mean + SD for symmetric data; median + IQR (Q₃ − Q₁) when skewed.

The normal distribution

The 68–95–99.7 rule

68%

of values lie within ±1σ of the mean

95%

lie within ±2σ

99.7%

lie within ±3σ

z = (x − μ) / σ measures distance from the mean in SDs · The rule describes individual measurements, not confidence intervals

Probability and inference

Confidence intervals

Standard error

SE(x̄) = s / √n

Sample-to-sample uncertainty of an estimate. Larger samples shrink it.

95% confidence interval

x̄ ± t · s / √n

The procedure covers the true μ in about 95% of repeated samples. One interval is not guaranteed.

Hypothesis testing

State the question first

H₀

Null hypothesis

The reference claim, e.g. a battery’s stated mean life.

μ = 12 hours

Hₐ

Alternative hypothesis

The difference being investigated (two-sided here).

μ ≠ 12 hours

A one-sided alternative (μ < 12) must be chosen in advance. Picking the direction after seeing the data distorts the test.

The six steps of a valid test

1

Define

The population, the parameter and the research question.

2

Hypotheses and α

Write H₀ and Hₐ; set α (often 0.05) before analysis.

3

Choose a test

Based on variable type and study design.

4

Check assumptions

Independence, normality, equal variance or expected counts.

5

Compute

Get the statistic and p-value; compare p with α.

6

Conclude

In context, with effect size and limitations.

p ≤ α → reject H₀ (data are sufficiently incompatible with H₀) · p > α → fail to reject H₀ (not proved true)

The p-value, visualised

Assuming H₀ and the test’s assumptions, the probability of a result at least as extreme as ours.

Not the probability that H₀ is true, and not the size of an effect.

Two-sided z = 2.00 → p ≈ 0.0455, past ±1.96: reject. Yet p = 0.055 would not be rejected, with similar evidence.

Two ways to be wrong

H₀ is actually true

H₀ is actually false

We reject H₀

We fail to reject H₀

Type I error (α)

False positive: a “discovery” that isn’t real

Correct ✓ Power (1 − β)

We detect a real effect

Correct decision

No effect, and we don’t claim one

Type II error (β)

False negative: we miss a real effect

P(Type I) = α · Power = 1 − β, and it grows with sample size, effect size and measurement precision

Multiple comparisons

The multiple-testing trap

64.2%

Chance of at least one false positive when 20 true nulls are each tested at α = 0.05

1 − (1 − 0.05)²⁰ ≈ 0.642

Report every planned comparison and consider adjustments.

Four major statistical tests

Z-test

A mean with known σ, or a proportion

z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ/√n)

μ₀ = 100 h, x̄ = 104, σ = 12, n = 36 → z = 2.00, p ≈ 0.046: reject

T-test

Means with σ estimated; one-sample, paired or Welch

t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s/√n)

x̄ = 52, s = 4, n = 16, μ₀ = 50 → t(15) = 2.00, p ≈ 0.064: fail to reject

Chi-square

Categorical counts: goodness of fit or independence

χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E

2 × 2 table, every E = 25 → χ² = 4.00, p ≈ 0.046; Cramér’s V = 0.20

ANOVA

Three or more independent group means

F = MS between / MS within

Means 70, 75, 80 → F(2, 27) ≈ 6.94, p ≈ 0.004; η² ≈ 0.34

Same idea every time: how far the data sit from H₀, relative to the variation expected by chance.

Same statistic, different verdict

Z-test · σ known

z = 2.00, n = 36 → p ≈ 0.0455

Reject H₀: μ = 100 h. The 95% CI [100.08, 107.92] just excludes 100.

T-test · σ estimated

t = 2.00, df = 15 → p ≈ 0.064

Fail to reject H₀: μ = 50. The 95% CI [49.87, 54.13] includes 50.

Why? Estimating σ from only 16 values gives the t distribution heavier tails, so the same 2.00 is less surprising.

Selecting a test

Research questionInitial choice
One mean, known population σOne-sample z-test
One mean, unknown σOne-sample t-test
Two independent meansWelch t-test
Before/after paired valuesPaired t-test
Two categorical variablesChi-square independence / exact test
Observed counts vs stated probabilitiesChi-square goodness-of-fit
Three or more group meansOne-way ANOVA / Welch ANOVA

The choice starts with the type of outcome and whether observations are independent.

Reporting a result

A complete result

“A two-sided one-sample t-test did not find sufficient evidence that the mean score differed from 50, t(15) = 2.00, p = 0.064. The sample mean was 52, and the 95% CI was [49.87, 54.13].”

Test · n · statistic · df · p-value · direction · CI or effect size

Three common errors

Reading p as the probability that H₀ is true

Changing the alternative after seeing the outcome

Treating repeated measurements as independent

Never say the null was “proved”, and never present an association as causation.

Final perspective

Key takeaways

01

Statistics begins with clear measurements and defensible data collection.

02

A test asks whether data are compatible with a claim; p is not the chance H₀ is true.

03

Explain what the evidence supports, and what it does not: report intervals and effect sizes.
